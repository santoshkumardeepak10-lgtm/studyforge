1. Real Numbers

Use prime factorisation and Euclid's algorithm to work with HCF and LCM.

Important ideas

  • Euclid's division lemma: a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.
  • Repeated division can be used to find HCF.
  • For positive integers a and b: HCF × LCM = a × b.
  • Prime factorisation gives the HCF and LCM.
  • Numbers such as √2 and √3 are irrational.
Example:

Find HCF of 225 and 135.

225 = 135 × 1 + 90
135 = 90 × 1 + 45
90 = 45 × 2 + 0

Therefore, HCF = 45.
Common mistake: In Euclid's algorithm, continue until the remainder becomes zero. The last non-zero remainder is the HCF.
Exam tip: Write every division step clearly.

2. Polynomials

Understand zeroes and their relationship with coefficients.

For ax² + bx + c

Sum of zeroes = −b/a

Product of zeroes = c/a
Example:

For 2x² − 7x + 3, Sum = 7/2
Product = 3/2
Common mistake: The sum of zeroes has a negative sign: −b/a.

3. Pair of Linear Equations

Solve two equations using substitution, elimination or graphical methods.

Consistency conditions

a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ → unique solution

a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ → no solution

a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ → infinitely many solutions
Example:

2x + y = 7
x − y = 2

Adding both equations: 3x = 9
x = 3

Therefore y = 1.

4. Quadratic Equations

Solve ax² + bx + c = 0 using factorisation or the quadratic formula.

Discriminant

D = b² − 4ac

D > 0 → two distinct real roots
D = 0 → two equal real roots
D < 0 → no real roots
Example:

x² − 5x + 6 = 0

(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0

x = 2 or x = 3

5. Arithmetic Progressions

An AP has a constant common difference.

aₙ = a + (n − 1)d

Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d]
Example:

7, 11, 15, 19,... a = 7
d = 4

10th term: a₁₀ = 7 + 9(4) = 43

6. Triangles

Similarity and proportionality are the main ideas in this chapter.

Similarity criteria

  • AA similarity
  • SSS similarity
  • SAS similarity

Important result

Corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional.

If ΔABC ~ ΔDEF and AB = 6, DE = 9, EF = 12, then BC/EF = AB/DE

BC/12 = 6/9
BC = 8

7. Coordinate Geometry

Use formulas systematically and keep the coordinates in the correct order.

Distance: √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Section formula: ((mx₂ + nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m+n))

Area: 1/2 |x₁(y₂−y₃)+x₂(y₃−y₁)+x₃(y₁−y₂)|
Example:

Distance between (1,2) and (4,6): √[(4−1)²+(6−2)²] = √(9+16) = 5

8. Introduction to Trigonometry

Trigonometric ratios connect angles and sides of a right triangle.

sin A = Perpendicular/Hypotenuse

cos A = Base/Hypotenuse

tan A = Perpendicular/Base

tan A = sin A / cos A

Important identities

sin²A + cos²A = 1

1 + tan²A = sec²A

1 + cot²A = cosec²A

9. Applications of Trigonometry

Use a diagram first, then select the correct trigonometric ratio.

Basic approach

  • Draw the right triangle.
  • Mark the known angle and sides.
  • Identify perpendicular, base and hypotenuse.
  • Select sin, cos or tan.
  • Solve for the unknown.
Example:

If angle = 45° and horizontal distance = 20 m, tan45° = height/20

1 = height/20

height = 20 m

10. Circles

Focus on tangent properties.

Important theorems

  • The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
If PA and PB are tangents from P:

PA = PB

11. Areas Related to Circles

Break complicated figures into familiar shapes.

Important formulas

Area of circle = πr²

Circumference = 2πr

Sector area = (θ/360°)πr²

Arc length = (θ/360°)2πr
Tip: For shaded regions, calculate the larger area and subtract the unwanted part.

12. Surface Areas & Volumes

Break combined solids into simpler solids.

Important formulas

Cylinder: CSA = 2πrh

V = πr²h

Cone: l = √(r²+h²)
CSA = πrl
TSA = πr(l+r)

V = 4/3πr³

Hemisphere: CSA = 2πr²

13. Statistics

Grouped data can be analysed using mean, median and mode.

Mean

Mean = Σfᵢxᵢ / Σfᵢ

Assumed mean method: x̄ = a + Σfᵢdᵢ / Σfᵢ

Step deviation: x̄ = a + h(Σfᵢuᵢ / Σfᵢ)

Median

Median = l + [(n/2 − cf)/f]h

Mode

Mode = l + [(f₁−f₀)/(2f₁−f₀−f₂)]h

14. Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is.

P(E) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of equally likely outcomes

0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1

P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
Example:

For a fair die, probability of getting an even number: Favourable outcomes = {2,4,6} = 3 Total outcomes = 6

P(E) = 3/6 = 1/2

📐 Important Proofs & Theorems

Class 10 exam-ready proofs and important formula derivations.

PROOF 01

Euclid's Division Algorithm

Statement: For positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r such that

a = bq + r,   0 ≤ r < b

To find HCF(a,b), divide a by b. Then divide b by the remainder. Continue until the remainder becomes zero.

The last non-zero remainder is the HCF.

Therefore, Euclid's algorithm gives HCF.
PROOF 02

√2 is Irrational

Assume √2 is rational.

Then √2 = p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0, with no common factor.

Squaring:

2 = p²/q²
p² = 2q²

Therefore p² is even, so p is even. Let p = 2k.

Then:

4k² = 2q²
q² = 2k²

Thus q is also even. This contradicts the assumption that p and q have no common factor.

Hence √2 is irrational.
PROOF 03

√p is Irrational for a Prime p

Suppose √p = a/b in lowest form, where p is prime.

Squaring gives:

a² = pb²

Therefore p divides a². Since p is prime, p divides a. Let a = pk.

Substituting:

p²k² = pb²
b² = pk²

So p also divides b, contradicting that a/b is in lowest form.

Therefore √p is irrational.
PROOF 04

Sum of Zeroes of a Quadratic Polynomial

Let α and β be zeroes of

ax² + bx + c

The polynomial can be written as:

a(x−α)(x−β)

Expanding:

ax² − a(α+β)x + aαβ

Comparing with ax² + bx + c:

−a(α+β) = b

α+β = −b/a
Sum of zeroes = −b/a.
PROOF 05

Product of Zeroes of a Quadratic Polynomial

From:

a(x−α)(x−β) = ax² − a(α+β)x + aαβ

Comparing the constant term with c:

aαβ = c

αβ = c/a
Product of zeroes = c/a.
PROOF 06

Relation Between Roots and Coefficients

For ax² + bx + c = 0 with roots α and β:

α + β = −b/a

αβ = c/a

These follow directly by writing the equation in factorised form:

a(x−α)(x−β)=0
These relations are extremely useful in exam questions.
PROOF 07

nth Term of an AP

Consider the AP:

a, a+d, a+2d, a+3d, ...

First term = a.

Second term = a+d.

Third term = a+2d.

Therefore, the nth term contains d exactly n−1 times.

aₙ = a + (n−1)d
Hence, nth term of an AP = a + (n−1)d.
PROOF 08

Sum of First n Terms of an AP

Let:

S = a + (a+d) + (a+2d) + ... + [a+(n−1)d]

Write the same sum in reverse order:

S = [a+(n−1)d] + ... + (a+d) + a

Adding:

2S = n[2a+(n−1)d]

Therefore:

S = n/2[2a+(n−1)d]
Hence proved.
PROOF 09

Basic Proportionality Theorem

In ΔABC, let D lie on AB and E lie on AC such that DE ∥ BC. Prove:

AD/DB = AE/EC

Triangles ADE and BDE have the same altitude from E to AB. Therefore:

Area(ADE)/Area(BDE) = AD/DB

Similarly, triangles ADE and CDE have the same altitude from D to AC:

Area(ADE)/Area(CDE) = AE/EC

Triangles BDE and CDE have the same base DE and lie between the same parallels DE and BC. Hence their areas are equal.

Therefore:

AD/DB = AE/EC
Hence, Basic Proportionality Theorem is proved.
PROOF 10

Converse of Basic Proportionality Theorem

In ΔABC, D lies on AB and E lies on AC. Suppose:

AD/DB = AE/EC

Through D draw DE' parallel to BC meeting AC at E'.

By BPT:

AD/DB = AE'/E'C

But given:

AD/DB = AE/EC

Therefore E' and E represent the same point on AC.

Hence DE ∥ BC.
PROOF 11

Pythagoras Theorem

In right-angled ΔABC, let ∠C = 90°. Draw CD perpendicular to hypotenuse AB.

From similarity:

AC² = AD × AB

BC² = BD × AB

Adding:

AC² + BC² = AB(AD+BD)

Since AD + BD = AB:

AC² + BC² = AB²
Hence, Pythagoras theorem is proved.
PROOF 12

Converse of Pythagoras Theorem

Suppose in ΔABC:

AB² = AC² + BC²

Construct a right triangle having sides AC and BC as its perpendicular sides. Let its hypotenuse be AD.

By Pythagoras:

AD² = AC² + BC²

But:

AB² = AC² + BC²

Therefore:

AD² = AB²
AD = AB

Thus the constructed right triangle has the same corresponding sides as ΔABC. Hence the angle between AC and BC is 90°.

Therefore the converse is proved.
PROOF 13

Area Ratio of Similar Triangles

If ΔABC ~ ΔDEF, then corresponding sides are proportional.

AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD

Area of triangles can be expressed using the same included-angle relationship. Therefore, if corresponding sides have ratio k, their areas have ratio k².

Area(ABC)/Area(DEF) = (AB/DE)²
Ratio of areas of similar triangles = square of ratio of corresponding sides.
PROOF 14

Distance Formula

Let A(x₁,y₁) and B(x₂,y₂). Draw a horizontal and vertical line to form a right triangle.

Horizontal distance:

|x₂−x₁|

Vertical distance:

|y₂−y₁|

Using Pythagoras:

AB² = (x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²

Taking square root:

AB = √[(x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²]
Distance formula derived.
PROOF 15

Section Formula

Let P(x,y) divide A(x₁,y₁) and B(x₂,y₂) internally in the ratio m:n.

By proportional division along the x-axis:

(x−x₁)/(x₂−x) = m/n

Cross multiplication gives:

n(x−x₁)=m(x₂−x)

Solving:

x = (mx₂ + nx₁)/(m+n)

Similarly:

y = (my₂ + ny₁)/(m+n)
Section formula is obtained.
PROOF 16

Area of Triangle Using Coordinates

For vertices:

A(x₁,y₁), B(x₂,y₂), C(x₃,y₃)

Using coordinate geometry and signed areas of the component triangles, the area simplifies to:

Area = 1/2 |x₁(y₂−y₃) + x₂(y₃−y₁) + x₃(y₁−y₂)|
If the area is zero, the three points are collinear.
PROOF 17

Fundamental Trigonometric Identity

In a right triangle, let perpendicular = P, base = B and hypotenuse = H.

By Pythagoras:

P² + B² = H²

Divide throughout by H²:

P²/H² + B²/H² = 1

Since:

P/H = sin A
B/H = cos A

Therefore:

sin²A + cos²A = 1
Hence proved.
PROOF 18

1 + tan²A = sec²A

Start with:

sin²A + cos²A = 1

Divide throughout by cos²A:

sin²A/cos²A + 1 = 1/cos²A

Using:

tan A = sin A/cos A
sec A = 1/cos A

Therefore:

tan²A + 1 = sec²A
Hence proved.
PROOF 19

1 + cot²A = cosec²A

Start with:

sin²A + cos²A = 1

Divide by sin²A:

1 + cos²A/sin²A = 1/sin²A

Using:

cot A = cos A/sin A
cosec A = 1/sin A

Therefore:

1 + cot²A = cosec²A
Hence proved.
PROOF 20

Tangent is Perpendicular to Radius

Let PT be tangent to a circle with centre O at T.

Draw OM perpendicular to PT.

If OM were smaller than OT, then M would lie inside the circle because OT is the radius. A line passing through an interior point of a circle meets the circle at two points.

But PT is a tangent and should meet the circle at only one point T. This is a contradiction.

Therefore OT ⟂ PT.
PROOF 21

Tangents from an External Point are Equal

Let PA and PB be tangents from external point P to a circle with centre O.

OA and OB are radii, so:

OA = OB

Also, radius is perpendicular to tangent:

∠OAP = ∠OBP = 90°

OP is common to both triangles.

Therefore:

ΔOAP ≅ ΔOBP

by RHS congruence.

Hence PA = PB.
PROOF 22

Complementary Probability Rule

Let E be an event and E' be its complement.

If total outcomes = n and favourable outcomes for E = m, then:

P(E) = m/n

The number of outcomes not favourable to E is n−m.

P(E') = (n−m)/n

Therefore:

P(E') = 1 − m/n
P(E') = 1 − P(E)
DERIVATION 23

Assumed Mean Formula

Let the assumed mean be a. For every observation:

dᵢ = xᵢ − a
xᵢ = a + dᵢ

Multiply by frequency fᵢ:

fᵢxᵢ = fᵢa + fᵢdᵢ

Adding all values:

Σfᵢxᵢ = aΣfᵢ + Σfᵢdᵢ

Divide by Σfᵢ:

x̄ = a + Σfᵢdᵢ/Σfᵢ
Assumed mean formula derived.
DERIVATION 24

Step-Deviation Formula

Let:

uᵢ = (xᵢ−a)/h

Therefore:

xᵢ = a + huᵢ

Multiply by fᵢ and add:

Σfᵢxᵢ = aΣfᵢ + hΣfᵢuᵢ

Dividing by Σfᵢ:

x̄ = a + h(Σfᵢuᵢ/Σfᵢ)
Step-deviation formula derived.
